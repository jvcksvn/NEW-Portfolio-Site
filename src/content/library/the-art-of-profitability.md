**The Art of Profitability**, Adrian Slywotzky, 2002.

Twenty three profit models delivered as a parable, and the parable is scaffolding: a fictional student repeats back what the professor already said. I read it anyway, for the taxonomy, which a spreadsheet could have delivered in one evening.

Slywotzky argues that profit is patterned rather than earned by effort alone: firms that study where money actually concentrates (installed bases, pyramids of price sensitivity, switchboards between many buyers and sellers) can redesign around a model instead of grinding inside an old one. The dialogue frames every model as a discovery the fictional executive stumbles into, which flattens twenty three genuinely different mechanisms into one repeatable epiphany that real businesses rarely get to have on schedule.

One line, because a taxonomy this long resists selection: always run the arithmetic before judging a plan, five calculations deep, before trusting the story attached to it. Everything else in the book is a label for a pattern rather than a transferable move, and labels do not survive being handed to someone else.

I ran the arithmetic test against a client proposal I was pricing for a quarterly retainer, roughly forty thousand dollars over three months. Five calculations in, the multi component idea the book spends thirty pages narrating turned out to be one line on a spreadsheet: which piece of the retainer was subsidizing which. That took twenty minutes, not three evenings. What changed is not the pricing, which was already fine. It is that I now open a business book's index before its introduction, looking for the model, and skip the chapter built to make me feel like I discovered it myself. Two books since have failed that test in the first ten pages and gone back on the shelf unfinished. One of those two was probably judged too fast; a colleague swears by it, and I still have not gone back to check.
