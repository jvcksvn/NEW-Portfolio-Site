**The Ultimate Sales Machine**, Chet Holmes, 2007.

A manual for selling to a buyer who still takes unsolicited calls and defers to a stranger's expertise by default. The systems for building repeatable process are sound. The buyer the scripts assume has mostly stopped existing.

Holmes argues that dominance comes from mastering a small number of disciplines and drilling each to permanence through a standing weekly meeting, rather than chasing new tactics one after another. Training, hiring, and time management get identical treatment: repeat until it is policy. The buyer facing half of the system, an adversarial hiring interview and the assumption that prospects want a salesperson's expertise handed to them unprompted, gets the same confidence as the operational half, and it has aged badly.

One thing, and this is the whole list: the weekly hour dedicated to a single narrow part of the business, because a recurring meeting with a fixed scope is the only format here that actually rebuilds a process instead of just discussing it.

What the book actually changed is how I read the rest of the genre. I now open a sales or negotiation book by checking whether its core script assumes the other side has less information than they actually carry, a cold call, a scarcity pitch, a stranger's unearned authority. I ran that check against two other business books this year. One held up. One did not, and I put it down forty pages in for the reason I could not use most of this one either: both were written for a market that had not yet learned to look things up before answering the phone.
