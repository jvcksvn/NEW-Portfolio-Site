**The Practice**, Seth Godin, 2020.

A quotable book of permission slips, true in each sentence and resistant to being organized into a method. Two hours produce maybe five ideas worth keeping, and a real risk that reading feels enough like the practice to replace it.

Godin argues the work itself, not the outcome, is the only legitimate object of judgment: ship on schedule, promise the shipping and not the result, and treat talent as a myth next to earned, practiced skill. Professionalism (getting paid, choosing a genre, finding the smallest audience that actually wants the work) supplies the structure that makes daily practice possible. The reversal of "do what you love" into "love what you do" is tidy, and does not survive work someone hates.

Two things. The smallest viable audience: make the thing for someone specific, not everyone, which turns a vague ambition into a short list of people whose reaction actually matters. And the separation of the promise from the result: the only commitment worth making is to ship on the date, not to make something good, because whether the result is good was never within anyone's control to promise in the first place.

I ship a five hundred word draft to three named readers every Friday by noon, whether I think it is any good or not, a streak now twenty two weeks long. Nineteen Fridays went out on time. The three that did not were the weeks I let myself reread before sending, which is precisely the outcome checking the book tells a person to cut and which I cut everywhere except, apparently, the weeks it would have mattered most. The smallest viable audience part has held better than the shipping part; all three readers still open the draft, and one has started forwarding it, which was never the goal and is the only actual result worth reporting.
