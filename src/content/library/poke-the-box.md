**Poke the Box**, Seth Godin, 2011.

A permission slip more than a method. It never explains how to tell a start worth making from noise, and it assumes you can absorb the cost of failure without much friction, which is not true for everyone reading it.

Godin argues that initiative, not talent or resources, is the scarce input in most careers, and that starting is a habit built through repetition rather than a decision made once and kept. Waiting for permission or certainty is treated as functionally identical to producing nothing at all. He is less convincing on the difference between projects: an unstarted idea and a shipped failure are not actually the same cost, whatever the book implies, and it never separates them.

Two things kept. Starting is a series of events rather than a single one, so the relevant measure is not whether today's attempt looks impressive but whether tomorrow's attempt happens at all. And a short list of what is not allowed feels safer than a blank page, which is a reason to distrust that comfort rather than to seek it out.

Any project idea I write down now gets a hard rule: something ships within seven days, even if it is a single ugly page, or the idea gets deleted rather than filed. Nineteen ideas have gone through the rule since I started it. Eleven shipped something small on time. Six got deleted on day seven, which I count as a result rather than a loss, since they used to sit in a folder for months instead. Two are still open past their deadline because I kept extending it, which is exactly the failure mode the rule was supposed to prevent, and I have not fixed that part yet.
