**The War of Art**, Steven Pressfield, 2002.

A pep talk with real teeth and one blind spot. Resistance names procrastination's felt weight well, but treats every stalled project as a failure of nerve, generous to the finisher, unfair to whoever is stuck on something structural instead.

Pressfield argues that creative avoidance is not a personal failing but a near universal force, Resistance, that scales with how much a piece of work matters to the person avoiding it. The fix is not inspiration but professionalism, showing up on a schedule and treating the work as a job rather than an identity. The later chapters lean on angels and muses as literal sources of ideas, a mystical framing the first two thirds do not need.

Two things kept. Resistance scales with how much a project matters, so the task I am avoiding most is diagnostic rather than incidental, a pointer to what actually counts. And the amateur professional split is about identity rather than income: the amateur needs the work to mean something about who they are, the professional treats it as a job that gets done whether or not the feeling shows up.

I keep a folder of drafts I am not finishing and once a week, Sunday night, I rank them by how much I am avoiding each one, then start Monday on whichever one I dread most. Six weeks of doing this, the ranking correctly flagged the two pieces that actually mattered, both finished and both published since. It also flagged a client email I had been avoiding for reasons that had nothing to do with importance, a question I could not answer because I was missing information, and the ranking could not tell that apart from an important draft. Pressfield's book cannot tell them apart either.
