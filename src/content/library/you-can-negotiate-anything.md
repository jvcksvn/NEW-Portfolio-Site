**You Can Negotiate Anything**, Herb Cohen, 1980.

A manual for one time adversarial encounters, from before anyone expected to face the same counterparty twice. Perception and power hold up. The scarcity and feigned indifference tactics assume the other side never finds out, and eventually it does.

Cohen argues that negotiating power is almost entirely a matter of perception rather than fact, and that whoever appears to hold more options, information, or indifference to the outcome controls the exchange. Real needs hide behind stated positions, so the work is extracting the other side's true limits while concealing your own. Later chapters extend this into open theater, dressing wealthy to borrow money, feigning confusion to draw out information, presented as cleverness rather than as a cost paid in trust.

One thing, and this is the whole list: separate the stated position from the real need, because most stalled negotiations are actually two unstated needs that were never put on the table.

What changed is how I read every negotiation book since. I now ask whether a tactic survives being said out loud to the other party, the entourage at the bank, the feigned confusion, because Cohen's book is full of moves that only work in the dark and depend on the other side never being told. I checked that question against two more recent negotiation books this year. Both explicitly discuss what happens when a tactic gets discovered, which is the exact test this one fails, and the reason it now reads like a period piece rather than a manual.
