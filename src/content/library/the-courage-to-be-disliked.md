**The Courage to be Disliked**, Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga, 2013.

A therapy session staged as a dialogue, and the staging oversells its own certainty: every problem interpersonal, every feeling chosen, full stop. Good for retelling an old wound differently; useless for a problem that is actually biological.

Kishimi and Koga argue, through Adler, that behavior is teleological rather than caused: a person first chooses a goal (staying home, provoking anger, avoiding a risk) and then manufactures the feeling and the story that justify it. Change follows from separating one's own tasks from everyone else's and trading the vertical scorekeeping of praise and blame for horizontal relationships built on contribution. The premise that trauma carries no causal weight at all is asserted more than it is earned.

Two things. The task separation question: whose task is this, tested by asking who actually receives the consequence of the choice. It turns a vague obligation into a decision about intervention. And the reframe of anger as equipment rather than weather; something reached for on purpose to produce a result, not something that happens to a person and then leaks out.

I run the task separation question before answering critical notes on the Tuesday design review, the one meeting where a client sits in the room while the work gets picked apart. Whose reaction is actually mine to manage; whose is not. Nine Tuesdays on it, the question holds cleanly when the critic is a stranger to the account and collapses the moment it is my business partner, because deciding that his reaction is his task is itself a decision he never agreed to be excluded from. The book has no chapter for a relationship two people are required to keep.
