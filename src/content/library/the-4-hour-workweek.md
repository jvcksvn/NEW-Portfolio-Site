**The 4 Hour Workweek**, Tim Ferriss, 2007.

A permission slip dressed as a system. Everything about elimination and negotiation is sharp and immediately usable. Everything about what to do with the freed time is thin, and the book quietly assumes a marketable skill and a boss willing to bend, which is not where most readers of it are starting from.

Ferriss argues that income and hours worked are unrelated once obligations are tested rather than accepted, and proposes measuring wealth in four variables (what is done, when, where, and with whom) instead of net worth. Mini retirements replace the single deferred payoff at the end of a career. Automation and delegation exist to remove the owner as the bottleneck. The tactics for negotiating remote work and firing bad customers are rigorous. The tactics for building something worth delegating in the first place are mostly assumed.

Two things, transferable. The distinction between effective and efficient: doing the right task badly beats doing the wrong task well, and most busyness is the second kind dressed as the first. And the elimination test, applied to a recurring obligation before optimizing it: ask what actually happens if it is simply removed, since most of what survives on a calendar does so out of inertia rather than necessity.

I ran the elimination test on a Friday status email sent to three stakeholders every week for over a year. I stopped sending it and watched. Five weeks in, two of the three never mentioned it. The third asked once, in week three, and now gets two lines instead of a page. A fourth person, one I had forgotten was even copied on it, noticed immediately and asked why he had been dropped, which is the part of the test the book underweights: some of what looks like inertia is actually someone quietly depending on the thing, and stopping is the only reliable way to find out which.
