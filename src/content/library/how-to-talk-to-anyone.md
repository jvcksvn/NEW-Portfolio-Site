**How to Talk to Anyone**, Leil Lowndes, 2003.

A phrasebook mistaken for a psychology. Roughly half the techniques work, and the book never explains why, which means it is impossible to tell in advance which half will age badly and which will not.

Lowndes argues that conversational success is a set of learnable, almost mechanical techniques, from timing a smile to mirroring a stranger's own vocabulary back at them, and that deploying enough of these correctly produces the warmth that naturally charismatic people appear to generate for free. Each technique gets a name and a script. Nothing in the book distinguishes a technique that builds real rapport from one that merely performs it, which matters, because several, read closely, are describing manipulation and calling it charm.

One line: match the other person's specific words back to them instead of substituting a paraphrase, since it is the one technique in the book with an honest mechanism behind it rather than just a catchy name.

What it changed is not how I talk, mostly. I tried four of the named techniques in real meetings over about six weeks and dropped three of them within days, the flooding smile in particular, which felt so calculated to perform that a colleague asked if I was feeling alright. What survived is the echoing technique, used maybe twice a week on client calls, and it has genuinely made people repeat themselves less and elaborate more. The larger result is that the book recalibrated how much I trust a technique that arrives with a proper name and no stated mechanism; I now assume the name is doing the work the evidence should be doing, and I read the next book in this genre checking for the difference.
