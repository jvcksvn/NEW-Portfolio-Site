**Steal Like An Artist**, Austin Kleon, 2012.

A permission slip, not a method. It tells someone stuck that copying is allowed and useful, which is true and worth hearing once. It has nothing to say about the day after permission is granted.

Kleon argues nothing is made from nothing: influence is the raw material of creative work, and the honest move is to copy openly, transform what is copied, and credit it, rather than pretend at originality. He treats constraint, routine, and a day job as accelerants rather than obstacles, and generosity (giving techniques away in public) as a growth strategy. The weak link is scale: he never distinguishes copying that builds skill from copying that just delays finding a voice.

Two things kept. The genealogy move: study one influence completely, then chase down three people that influence loved, and repeat until there is a family tree instead of a single hero. It replaces vague inspiration with a reading list. And the idea that a constraint is a door rather than a wall; a tight limitation forces a specific choice where an open field just produces paralysis. Both are really the same claim in different clothes: freedom in the small choices comes from removing freedom somewhere else first.

I ran the constraint move on the newsletter illustration that goes out every Thursday: one color, forty minutes, no reference open on a second monitor. Before that, the drawing routinely ate the whole morning while I looked for the right example to riff on. Twelve issues in, ten shipped with an illustration under the forty minute cap. The other two went out with no image at all, both weeks the deadline slid to Wednesday afternoon, which is exactly when a forty minute box stops being a constraint and starts being one more thing to skip. The genealogy exercise, meanwhile, has stalled completely; I made a list of three people my favorite illustrator stole from and have not opened it in months, which says more about follow through than about the idea.
