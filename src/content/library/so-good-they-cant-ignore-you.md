**So Good They Can't Ignore You**, Cal Newport, 2012.

A sharp corrective to follow your passion that assumes the market will pay you fairly for the capital you build. It says almost nothing about what to do when a skilled person hits a wall that skill alone does not move: bias, credentialing, or an employer who just says no.

Newport argues that passion is an output of skill and autonomy rather than an input you locate by searching, and that the craftsman question, what can I offer the world, produces better careers than the passion question, what does the world owe me. Skill, accumulated as career capital, is the only currency that reliably buys the traits, creativity, impact, control, that make work good. He is thinner on what happens when the capital is real but the market refuses to trade it for control anyway, a gap his own case studies mostly sidestep by featuring people whose capital did convert.

Two things kept. Control taken before you have the capital to pay for it collapses the first time it is tested, so the ask should come after the leverage exists, not before. And a mission is not found by introspection; it is found at the edge of a field you already know well, which means the actual task is getting to the edge rather than deciding what to look for once you are there.

I keep a running list next to my actual job title of any request for more autonomy, a four day week trial, picking my own projects, and each one has to wait until I can point to a specific, recently shipped thing that makes the ask cheap to grant. Three asks logged this year. Two got approved after I could point to a shipped project the request rode on: the four day trial and picking my next two projects. The third, asking to skip a recurring status meeting, got declined even with a project attached, because the meeting turned out not to be about my output at all, and no amount of career capital moves a meeting that was never really evaluating me.
